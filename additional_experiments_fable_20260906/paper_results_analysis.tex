\subsection{Data Analysis}

\autoref{fig:results_distribution} shows that the motion capture data cover the target command range of the robot only insufficiently, and the yaw command only in a skewed manner. AMP matches the state-transition distribution of the policy to that of the demonstrations via the style reward, whereas the task reward drives the policy towards the commanded velocity. For target commands without expert support, the two reward components therefore compete, and the downstream policy exhibits poor tracking in exactly these regions, i.e., for backward walking and for yaw rates that the demonstrations rarely contain. Our extended video dataset covers the target command range more broadly, raising the covered fraction of the command box from $13.2\%$ to $25.0\%$, in particular for slow, standing, and turning commands that are easy to record on video but absent from the MoCap data.

In \autoref{fig:deep_data_analytics}, we show that data coverage is linked to downstream policy performance and imitation quality. Intuitively, the closer a target command lies to a command observed in the expert frames, the better the tracking (rank correlation $\rho=0.61$ for MoCap and $0.31$ for Video (extended)), since task and style reward agree in that case. Because the AMP discriminator observes only the joint states ($q$, $\dot{q}$), commands far from the expert data additionally force the policy into state transitions that the discriminator has never seen as expert samples: the distance between the agent's joint states and the nearest expert frame grows with the tracking error ($\rho=0.38$ and $0.40$). Coverage of the command range by the expert data is thus a requirement for both tracking and imitation.

\subsection{Reconstruction Quality}

\autoref{fig:example_traj} compares the keypoint trajectories obtained from MoCap with those reconstructed from video. The video trajectories are visibly noisier: before filtering, the torso keypoints scatter by $2.6\times$ and the paw keypoints by $2.0\times$ the MoCap values around positions that should be stationary, measured as the RMS deviation of a paw from its mean position while it is planted and of the torso from its smooth path while the body is supported ($0.7\%$ and $2.4\%$ of hip height). The filter removes the high-frequency part of this noise: the residual of the paw keypoints drops from $12.8\,$mm to $3.4\,$mm and the spectral power above $5\,$Hz by a factor of six, while the motion itself is preserved, i.e., net displacement and stride frequency are unchanged, the ground-contact fraction changes from $44\%$ to $42\%$, and the peak paw clearance decreases by $6\%$. The remaining error is low-frequency, e.g., a scatter of the stance height of $2\%$ of hip height, and is not removed by a temporal filter. Despite this remaining noise, the policies trained on the extended video dataset learn more efficient gaits and better tracking than those trained on MoCap (\autoref{fig:results_metrics}).

Only $0.4\%$ of the depth measurements are rejected as outliers, whereas $27\%$ of the paw samples ($10$--$49\%$ per clip) are interpolated because a foot is occluded or the depth reading at its pixel is missing. Tracking the torso keypoints was reliable and needed minimal manual refinement, such as choosing slightly different initial pixels to start the tracker. Tracking the feet is less reliable during fast motion and long occlusions, and its quality depends on the initial keypoint selection; after long occlusions, such as of the side turned away from the camera in the stand-up motion, keypoints had to be re-selected within the clip.
